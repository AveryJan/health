# Pediatric Prehospital Assessment: 3-Year-Old Choking Patient

## Prompt 1: Age-Appropriate Vital Signs (1 Point)

For a 3-year-old pediatric patient, the expected vital sign ranges according to the assignment rubric are:

- **Systolic blood pressure:** 76–115 mmHg
- **Heart rate:** 85–140 beats per minute
- **Respiratory rate:** 22–30 breaths per minute

I would use these age-appropriate ranges when assessing the child's cardiovascular and respiratory status, recognizing that vital signs must be interpreted alongside the child's appearance, breathing effort, circulation, and clinical condition.

## Prompt 2: Potential Age-Appropriate Intervention (1 Point)

If the 3-year-old child has a severe airway obstruction and is conscious but unable to breathe, speak, or cough effectively, I would perform five firm back blows between the shoulder blades followed by five abdominal thrusts, repeating these cycles until the obstruction is relieved or the child becomes unresponsive. If the child becomes unresponsive, I would begin pediatric CPR, activate or confirm additional emergency assistance, and use appropriate pediatric airway equipment according to current resuscitation guidelines.

## Prompt 3: Additional Call-Specific or Age-Specific Consideration (1 Point)

Before arriving, I would review pediatric airway management and prepare appropriately sized equipment, including a pediatric bag-valve mask, suction device, oxygen delivery equipment, and airway adjuncts. I would also review pediatric CPR, including the 30:2 compression-to-ventilation ratio for a single rescuer, in case the patient becomes unresponsive. Because the patient is only 3 years old, I would anticipate possible fear or difficulty cooperating, use simple age-appropriate language, and involve the mother to provide reassurance and assist with obtaining the child's history. I would also remember that forceful coughing is generally encouraged when a child can still cough effectively, while severe airway obstruction requires immediate intervention.

## Prompt 4: Pediatric Assessment Triangle (2 Points)

**Appearance:** The child appears alert, active, and developmentally appropriate for her age, as evidenced by walking around, playing with toys, and interacting appropriately with her mother.

**Work of Breathing:** The child does not appear to be in respiratory distress because she is breathing at a normal rate without visible chest retractions, stridor, or other abnormal breathing sounds.

**Circulation to Skin:** The child appears to have adequate circulation because she is active and appropriately interactive, with no indication of ongoing pallor or cyanosis and a capillary refill time of less than two seconds on subsequent examination.

The overall pediatric assessment triangle suggests that the child is currently stable without obvious signs of airway compromise, respiratory failure, or poor perfusion. However, because she recently experienced a choking episode, I would continue monitoring for recurrent airway obstruction or possible foreign-body aspiration.

## Prompt 5: Patient Introduction and Communication (2 Points)

I would approach the child calmly, kneel to her eye level, smile gently, and speak in a friendly, reassuring tone. I would introduce myself to both the child and her mother, explain my role, and obtain permission before proceeding with the assessment.

**EMT:** "Hi! My name is Alex, and I'm an Emergency Medical Technician, or EMT. I'm here to help make sure you're feeling okay. What's your name?"

**Child:** "Emma."

**EMT:** "Hi, Emma! It's nice to meet you! I see you're playing with your toys. Which one is your favorite?"

**Child:** "My teddy bear!"

**EMT:** "That's a really nice teddy bear! Your mommy told me you had some trouble while eating. Are you feeling better now?"

**Child:** "Yes!"

**EMT:** "I'm happy to hear that! Would it be okay if I listened to your breathing and checked your heartbeat? I'll tell you what I'm doing, and your mommy can stay right next to you."

**To the mother:** "My name is Alex, and I'm an EMT. With your permission, I'd like to examine Emma and ask a few questions about what happened to make sure she's safe."

**Mother:** "Yes, that's fine."

**EMT:** "Thank you. Can you tell me what she was eating when the choking started, how long she was coughing, and whether she had any difficulty breathing or lost consciousness? Has she had any additional coughing, noisy breathing, or discomfort since the episode?"

**To the child:** "Emma, you're doing a wonderful job! I'm just going to listen to your chest with my stethoscope. It might feel a little cool, but it shouldn't hurt. You can hold your teddy bear while I check."

Throughout the interaction, I would use active listening, allow the mother to answer questions, respect the child's comfort level, and avoid sudden movements. I would explain each action before examining the child and provide positive reassurance.

**I would also smile warmly, kneel to the child's eye level, and use gentle facial expressions to further establish rapport with the patient.**

## Prompt 6: Hospital Handoff Report Using SBAR (2 Points)

**Demographic Information:** The patient is a 3-year-old female presenting after a witnessed choking episode.

**S — Situation:** We are transporting a 3-year-old female after a suspected choking episode involving a piece of bread that occurred shortly before her mother called 911. According to the mother, the child coughed for approximately a couple of minutes, possibly less, and appeared to clear the obstruction independently before EMS arrival. She is currently asymptomatic and behaving normally.

**B — Background:** The mother reports that the child briefly appeared pale during the episode but never lost consciousness and cried afterward. She is otherwise healthy, with no significant medical history, recent illness, medications, or known allergies.

**A — Assessment:** The patient is alert, playful, walking around, and interacting appropriately with her mother. Her vital signs are blood pressure 90/60 mmHg, heart rate 110 beats per minute, and respiratory rate 24 breaths per minute. She has a normal voice, no drooling or stridor, clear bilateral lung sounds, no wheezing or chest retractions, and capillary refill of less than two seconds. There is no current evidence of respiratory distress or ongoing complete airway obstruction.

**R — Recommendation/Response:** No specific interventions were required during our assessment, and the patient remained stable without developing new symptoms. Her mother requests evaluation in the emergency department. We recommend further assessment following the choking episode, including consideration of possible foreign-body aspiration, and continued monitoring for any changes in airway status, breathing, or circulation.

## Conclusion

This scenario emphasizes the importance of pediatric-specific preparation, rapid recognition of airway emergencies, systematic assessment using the pediatric assessment triangle, family-centered communication, and effective transfer of care through an SBAR handoff. Although the child appears clinically stable following spontaneous resolution of the choking episode, continued observation and emergency department evaluation are appropriate because a normal initial examination does not completely exclude the possibility of foreign-body aspiration.

**Age-Appropriate Vital Sign Ranges**

For a 3-year-old pediatric patient, the expected vital sign ranges are:

- **Heart rate:** 80–120 beats per minute
- **Respiratory rate:** 20–29 breaths per minute

These ranges provide a baseline for evaluating the child's cardiovascular and respiratory stability.

**Potential Age-Appropriate Intervention**

If the child has a severe airway obstruction and cannot breathe, speak, or cough effectively, I would administer five back blows followed by five abdominal thrusts, repeating the cycle until the obstruction is relieved or the child becomes unresponsive. If the child becomes unresponsive, I would initiate pediatric CPR according to current resuscitation guidelines.

**Additional Pre-Arrival Considerations**

Before arrival, I would prepare appropriately sized pediatric airway and resuscitation equipment, including a pediatric bag-valve mask, suction equipment, and oxygen delivery supplies. I would also consider the child's developmental stage and anticipate possible anxiety or fear associated with emergency responders. I would plan to involve the child's mother in the assessment to provide reassurance and obtain an accurate history.

## 2. Pediatric Assessment Triangle

**Appearance:** The child appears alert, active, and neurologically appropriate for her age, demonstrated by walking around, playing with toys, and interacting normally with her mother.

**Work of Breathing:** The child demonstrates normal respiratory effort, with no visible retractions, stridor, wheezing, or other signs of respiratory distress, suggesting that her airway is currently patent.

**Circulation to Skin:** The child appears to have adequate peripheral circulation, supported by a capillary refill time of less than two seconds, normal activity, and no reported persistent pallor or cyanosis.

**Overall Impression:** Based on the pediatric assessment triangle, the child appears clinically stable at the time of assessment, with no current evidence of severe airway obstruction, respiratory distress, or inadequate perfusion. However, continued monitoring is necessary because of the recent choking episode and the possibility of a retained or aspirated foreign body.
